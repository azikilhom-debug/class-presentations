DEFINITIONS Always start here

Experiment, sample space, event

Random experiment
A procedure with an uncertain result

Roll two dice. Call one customer. Wait for tomorrow's exchange rate.

Repeatable, at least in principle.
Sample space S
Every possible outcome, listed once

One die: S = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}. Two dice: 36 ordered pairs. A loan decision: S = {approve, reject}.

Outcomes must be exhaustive and mutually exclusive.
Event A
Any subset of S you care about

A = "the two dice sum to 7" is a set of six outcomes. An event happens when the realised outcome is inside it.

P(A) = favourable ÷ total, when outcomes are equally likely.

NOTATION Words to symbols

Three operations on events

A ∪ B · union
"A or B, or both"

In everyday speech "or" often means one but not the other. In probability it always includes both.

A ∩ B · intersection
"A and B together"

Also written A ∩ B or simply AB. If it is empty, the events are mutually exclusive.

A′ · complement
"not A"

Everything in S outside A. The single most useful trick in the whole topic.

THE FOUNDATION · KOLMOGOROV, 1933

All of probability rests on three axioms

Axiom 1 · range
0 ≤ P(A) ≤ 1

A probability is never negative and never above one. "A 120% chance" is not a strong claim, it is a false one.

Axiom 2 · certainty
P(S) = 1

Something in the sample space must happen. The probabilities of all outcomes sum to exactly one.

Axiom 3 · addition
P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B)

…provided A and B cannot both happen. Everything else today is derived from this one line.

Exam habit: before computing anything, check that the numbers you were given satisfy the axioms. Inconsistent data is a common trick question.

WHERE NUMBERS COME FROM Three legitimate sources

How do you get a probability at all?

Classical
Count the symmetric cases
P(A) = favourable ÷ total

Dice, cards, a fair lottery. Requires equally likely outcomes — which almost no business problem gives you.

Relative frequency
Count what happened before
P(A) ≈ times A occurred ÷ trials

260 of 400 loans approved → P(approval) ≈ 0.65. The workhorse of applied statistics, and what the whole course assumes.

Subjective
A considered degree of belief
"70% chance the rate is cut"

Legitimate when there is no repeatable trial — a new product, a policy change. Must still obey all three axioms.

RULE 1 OF 3 The laziest useful rule

The complement rule

P(A′) = 1 − P(A)

Direct from axioms 2 and 3: A and A′ cannot both happen, and together they fill S.

Use it whenever counting the thing you want is harder than counting the thing you don't.
Worked · call centre

Historically 8% of calls are abandoned before an agent answers. Four independent calls arrive.

P(at least one abandoned)
= 1 − P(none abandoned)
= 1 − (0.92)4
= 1 − 0.716
0.284

Listing every way "at least one" can happen takes fifteen cases. The complement takes one line.

RULE 2 OF 3 "Or" questions

The addition rule

P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B)

Worked · 200 bank customers
Hold a debit card (D)
120
Hold a credit card (C)
60
Hold both (D ∩ C)
30
P(D) = 120/200 = 0.60
P(C) = 60/200 = 0.30
P(D ∩ C) = 30/200 = 0.15
One customer picked at random
P(D ∪ C) = 0.60 + 0.30 − 0.15
0.75

Adding 0.60 and 0.30 alone gives 0.90 — it counts the 30 customers with both cards twice. Subtracting the overlap once fixes it.

And by the complement rule, P(neither card) = 1 − 0.75 = 0.25, i.e. 50 customers.

READING THE TABLE It is all about the denominator

Joint, marginal, conditional

Marginal
One event, whole sample
P(A) = 260/400 = 0.65
P(T) = 240/400 = 0.60

Divide a margin total by the grand total.

Joint
Both events, whole sample
P(A ∩ T) = 168/400 = 0.42
P(A′ ∩ T′) = 68/400 = 0.17

Divide an inner cell by the grand total.

Conditional
One event, given the other
P(A | T) = 168/240 = 0.70
P(A | T′) = 92/160 = 0.575

The condition shrinks the sample space to one row. Full treatment next class.

Notice what just happened: knowing the applicant is from Tashkent moved the approval probability from 0.65 to 0.70. Information changed the number — so the two events are not independent.

RULE 3 OF 3 "And" questions

The multiplication rule and independence

General form · always true
P(A ∩ B) = P(A) · P(B | A)

Read it as a sequence: A happens, and then B happens given that A did.

Independent form · only if independent
P(A ∩ B) = P(A) · P(B)

A and B are independent when knowing one tells you nothing about the other: P(B | A) = P(B).

Test our loan table
If A and T were independent:
P(A) · P(T) = 0.65 × 0.60 = 0.39
The table actually shows:
P(A ∩ T) = 0.42
0.42 ≠ 0.39 → not independent

Region carries information about approval. Whether the gap is large enough to be more than sampling noise is a question for the chi-square test in week 11 — this is only the arithmetic.

THE CLASSIC CONFUSION Examined every year

Mutually exclusive ≠ independent

MUTUALLY EXCLUSIVE
INDEPENDENT
The question it answers
Can both happen at the same time?
Does one carry information about the other?
Defining equation
P(A ∩ B) = 0
P(A ∩ B) = P(A) · P(B)
Example
One loan is approved / rejected
Two different customers default
Can they hold together?
Only if one of the events has probability zero. If P(A) > 0 and P(B) > 0 and they are mutually exclusive, they are necessarily dependent.
Learning that A occurred tells you B did not — that is information, so independence fails.
