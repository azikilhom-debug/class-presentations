Three rules, and the gap they leave

Complement
P(A′) = 1 − P(A)
Addition
P(A∪B) = P(A)+P(B)−P(A∩B)
Multiplication

The gap

Every classical probability is favourable ÷ total. Both numbers still have to be counted.

COUNTING · 1 OF 3 Everything else follows from this

The multiplication principle

If a task has k stages, and stage i can be done in ni ways, the whole task can be done in n1 × n2 × … × nk ways.

Bank PIN
4 digits, repetition allowed
10 × 10 × 10 × 10
10 000

P(guessing on one try) = 0.0001.

Car plate
2 letters then 3 digits
26 × 26 × 10 × 10 × 10
676 000

Stages need not have the same size.

Order of coffee
3 sizes × 4 milks × 2 syrups
3 × 4 × 2
24

The café menu claims "hundreds of combinations". Check the arithmetic.

COUNTING · 2 OF 3 Order matters

Permutations

P(n, k) = n! (n − k)!

Choosing k items out of n and arranging them. No repetition.

n! = n × (n−1) × … × 1, and 0! = 1 by definition — there is exactly one way to arrange nothing.
Worked · student council

Twelve candidates. Three distinct posts: chair, treasurer, secretary. How many outcomes?

P(12, 3) = 12! ÷ 9!
= 12 × 11 × 10
1 320

Anvar–Dilnoza–Rustam is a different council from Rustam–Anvar–Dilnoza. That is what "order matters" means in practice.

COUNTING · 3 OF 3 Order does not matter

Combinations

C(n, k) = n! k! (n − k)!

The same selections, with the k! orderings of each group divided out. Also written n choose k.

Always C(n, k) ≤ P(n, k), and C(n, k) = C(n, n−k) — choosing 4 to include is the same as choosing 8 to exclude.
Same twelve candidates

Now a working group of three, all with equal standing. How many groups?

C(12, 3) = 1 320 ÷ 3!
= 1 320 ÷ 6
220

Six times fewer, because the six orderings of each trio are now the same group.

WORKED EXAMPLE Counting inside a probability

A quality inspection, and a lottery

Both are classical probabilities: count the favourable sets, count all sets, divide.

Inspection

A crate holds 20 units, 5 of them defective. An inspector draws 4 at random, without replacement.

Total samples: C(20, 4) = 4 845
All good: C(15, 4) = 1 365
P(no defective) = 1365 / 4845 = 0.282
P(at least one defective) = 1 − 0.282 = 0.718. The complement rule again — 71.8% of inspections catch something.
Lottery · 6 from 49

Order of the drawn balls is irrelevant, so this is one combination out of all of them.

C(49, 6) = 49! ÷ (6! · 43!)
= 13 983 816
P(jackpot on one ticket) = 0.000 000 072
Buying 100 tickets a week for 50 years leaves the probability of ever winning at about 1.9%.

THE PATTERN WORTH MEMORISING

Rare events stop being rare when you repeat them

P(at least one in n independent trials) = 1 − (1 − p)n
p = 0.02 · n = 1
2%
n = 10
18%
n = 50
64%
n = 200
98%

A 2% default rate per loan is comfortable. A portfolio of 200 such loans defaults somewhere almost certainly — and pricing that is the entire business of a credit department.

HOUR 2 · R BEGINS Open posit.cloud or RStudio now

From this class on, every topic ends in R

Why R and not a spreadsheet

A script is a record. Anyone — including you in June — can rerun it and get the identical number. A spreadsheet cannot be audited that way.

Why R and not a calculator

C(49, 6) is one keystroke. A million simulated experiments take under a second. Both are impossible by hand.

What you need

Nothing installed: posit.cloud in a browser is enough. Free account, works on a phone in a pinch.

R is examined the way arithmetic is: you will be asked what a short script returns, and to write four or five lines that answer a question. No package beyond base R is required in this course.

Read it out loud

R · SESSION 1 When the formula is hard

Simulation: probability by brute force

Relative frequency, taken literally: run the experiment 100 000 times and count.

set.seed(2026) # reproducible randomness
d1 <- sample(1:6, 100000, replace = TRUE)
d2 <- sample(1:6, 100000, replace = TRUE)
total <- d1 + d2
mean(total == 7) # 0.16644
mean(total > 9) # 0.16721
# exact answers from slide 5 of class 1:
# 6/36 = 0.16667 · 6/36 = 0.16667
Three things to notice

total == 7 produces 100 000 TRUE/FALSE values; mean() of those is the proportion of TRUEs. That idiom appears in every simulation you will write.

set.seed() fixes the random stream so your result is reproducible. Without it your answer changes on every run.

Simulation gets close, never exact. More runs, closer answer — the precise statement of that is the Central Limit Theorem in week 7.

The one to remember

This returns exactly the values beyond the 1.5 × IQR fences we computed by hand last week. R applies the same rule — it does not know more than you do.

SURVIVAL GUIDE The five errors everyone hits today

When R complains

WHAT YOU SEE
WHAT IT MEANS
could not find function "lenght"
A typo. R is case-sensitive and spelling-sensitive: Mean() is not mean().
object 'rev2' not found
You used a name you never assigned — or you assigned it in a line you did not actually run.
+ (the console will not return)
An unclosed bracket or quote. Press Esc and retype the line.
NA returned by mean()
A missing value in the data. Use mean(x, na.rm = TRUE) — and say in the write-up how many were missing.
a different answer every run
Simulation without set.seed(). Not an error — but not reproducible, so not acceptable in a submission.
