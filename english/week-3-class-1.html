THE DEFINITION Conditioning shrinks the sample space

Conditional probability

P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) P(B)

Defined whenever P(B) > 0. Read the bar as "given": the probability of A, in a world where B is already known to have happened.

The denominator is no longer the whole sample space. B is the new sample space, and A ∩ B is the favourable part of it.
Before and after
Sample space S — P(A ∩ B) is a small slice of everything.
Given B, only B exists — the same slice is now a large share of it.

Nothing about the world changed. Only what we know changed — and so the probability did.

THE ERROR THIS WHOLE CLASS EXISTS TO PREVENT

P(A | B) is not P(B | A)

Almost everyone accepts
P(has four legs | is a dog) ≈ 1

True, and unremarkable.

Nobody would then say
P(is a dog | has four legs) ≈ 1

Obviously false — most four-legged things are not dogs.

With animals the inversion is obvious. With a fraud alert, a medical test or a credit score it is invisible — and it is made in newspapers, courtrooms and boardrooms every week. Bayes' theorem is the tool that converts one into the other correctly.

REARRANGED Sequences without replacement

The multiplication rule, chained

P(A ∩ B) = P(A) · P(B | A)   →   P(A ∩ B ∩ C) = P(A) · P(B | A) · P(C | A ∩ B)

Worked · audit sampling

A file drawer holds 20 contracts, 5 of which are non-compliant. The auditor pulls two, without replacement.

P(1st compliant) = 15/20 = 0.75
P(2nd compliant | 1st was) = 14/19 = 0.737
P(both compliant) = 0.75 × 0.737
0.553
Why not 0.75 × 0.75?

Because the first draw changed the drawer. Nineteen contracts remain, fourteen of them compliant. Drawing without replacement makes the draws dependent.

With replacement — put the contract back — the draws are independent and 0.75 × 0.75 = 0.5625 is right.
Rule of thumb: when the population is large relative to the sample, the difference is negligible and we treat draws as independent. That approximation runs through weeks 7–11.

P(B) = P(B | A1)P(A1) + P(B | A2)P(A2) + … + P(B | Ak)P(Ak)

Two conditions on the branches

Mutually exclusive — a unit comes from exactly one supplier.

Exhaustive — the shares sum to 1, so no unit is unaccounted for.

Together this is called a partition of the sample space. Without it the weighted average is meaningless.

Note what the 4% hides: supplier C ships 20% of units but produces 45% of the defects. The next slide asks exactly that question backwards.
THE THEOREM Reversing the condition

Bayes' theorem

P(A | B) = P(B | A) · P(A) P(B)

It is not a new law. Write P(A ∩ B) two ways — P(A)P(B|A) and P(B)P(A|B) — set them equal, divide. Three lines of algebra.

The denominator P(B) is almost always computed by the law of total probability:
P(B) = P(B|A)P(A) + P(B|A′)P(A′)
The four names
P(A) · prior
What you believed before the evidence — the base rate.
P(B | A) · likelihood
How probable the evidence is if A is true.
P(B) · evidence
How probable the evidence is overall.
P(A | B) · posterior
What you should believe now.

Bayes is the arithmetic of changing your mind by exactly the right amount.

WORKED EXAMPLE The supplier question, reversed

A defective unit arrives. Who sent it?

We know P(defective | supplier). The warranty desk needs P(supplier | defective).

Step by step, for supplier C
Prior: P(C) = 0.20
Likelihood: P(D | C) = 0.09
Evidence: P(D) = 0.04 (slide 8)
P(C | D) = (0.09 × 0.20) ÷ 0.04
= 0.018 ÷ 0.04
0.45
All three posteriors
P(A | D) = 0.010 / 0.040.25
P(B | D) = 0.012 / 0.040.30
P(C | D) = 0.018 / 0.040.45
Sum1.00

Posteriors over a partition always sum to 1 — your arithmetic check.

Supplier C is a fifth of the volume and nearly half the defects. That single number is what the procurement meeting is about.

The same thing in symbols
P(F) = 0.001 · P(+|F) = 0.99 · P(+|F′) = 0.01
P(+) = 0.99(0.001) + 0.01(0.999) = 0.01098

0.090

Nine per cent. The test is excellent; fraud is simply so rare that false alarms, drawn from a vastly larger honest population, outnumber true hits eleven to one.

The evidence did work: 0.001 → 0.090 is a ninetyfold increase in probability. It just started from very far away.

R · SESSION 2 Conditional probability by machine

Contingency tables in R

# the week-2 loan table, entered directly
loans <- matrix(c(168, 72, 92, 68), nrow = 2,
  byrow = TRUE,
  dimnames = list(region = c("Tashkent", "Other"),
              decision = c("Approved", "Rejected")))
addmargins(loans) # row and column totals
# joint probabilities — divide by 400
prop.table(loans)
# conditional on the ROW: P(decision | region)
prop.table(loans, margin = 1)
# Approved Rejected
# Tashkent 0.700 0.300
# Other 0.575 0.425
The margin argument is the whole idea
prop.table(t) — joint, ÷ grand total
margin = 1 — rows sum to 1: P(col | row)
margin = 2 — columns sum to 1: P(row | col)

Choosing the margin is choosing the denominator — the same decision you make by hand, now one argument.

With raw data instead of counts: table(region, decision) builds the same object from two columns of a data frame.
R · SESSION 2 Your first function

Bayes as a reusable function

bayes <- function(prior, sens, fpr) {
  evidence <- sens * prior + fpr * (1 - prior)
  (sens * prior) / evidence
}
# the fraud alert from slide 11
bayes(prior = 0.001, sens = 0.99, fpr = 0.01)
# 0.09016
# rarer fraud — the quiz question
bayes(0.0001, 0.99, 0.01) # 0.00980
# how the answer moves with the base rate
p <- c(0.0001, 0.001, 0.01, 0.05, 0.20)
round(bayes(p, 0.99, 0.01), 3)
# 0.010 0.090 0.500 0.839 0.961
Two things this shows

Functions. Write the logic once, name it, reuse it. Anything you compute more than twice should be a function.

Vectorisation. Pass a vector of five priors and get five answers — no loop. This is why R feels different from other languages.

Read the last line as a sentence: the same test is worthless at a base rate of 1 in 10 000, a coin flip at 1%, and near-certain at 20%. The test never changed.
R · SESSION 2 Checking the theorem without the theorem

Simulating the fraud alert

set.seed(2026)
n <- 200000
# 1. is the transaction fraudulent?
fraud <- runif(n) < 0.001
# 2. does the system flag it?
flag <- ifelse(fraud,
runif(n) < 0.99, # caught
runif(n) < 0.01) # false alarm
# 3. among flagged, what share is fraud?
mean(fraud[flag]) # 0.0894
table(fraud, flag)
Line 3 is conditioning
fraud[flag]

"Keep only the flagged ones, then look at fraud." That square bracket is the shrunken sample space from slide 4 — R conditioning is literally subsetting.

0.0894 against the exact 0.0902. The gap is simulation noise, and it shrinks as n grows.

Use this technique when you distrust your own algebra: build the world, count, compare. If the two disagree, one of them is wrong and you now know to look.

EXAM CLINIC Where the marks go

Five mistakes, every year

THE MISTAKE
THE FIX
Reporting P(B | A) when asked for P(A | B)
Underline the given in the question before writing anything. The word after "given" is the denominator.
Dividing by the grand total in a conditional
Conditioning shrinks the sample space. Divide by the row or column total, never by n.
Ignoring the base rate
A likelihood alone is never an answer. Ask "out of how many, in total?" and use natural frequencies.
Assuming independence to make it easier
Test it: P(A ∩ B) = P(A)P(B)? Without replacement, or with any shared cause, it usually fails.
Forgetting the posteriors must sum to 1
Over a partition they always do. It costs five seconds and catches most arithmetic slips.
